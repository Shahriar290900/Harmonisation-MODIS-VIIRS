1. What Parallax Fire is

Parallax Fire shows how many fire detections two NASA satellites recorded, month by month, for 3 checked places (Bangladesh, New South Wales, California) and the divisions and states inside them. The data runs from 2001 to 2025.

The two satellites do not count the same way. The older one (MODIS) sees squares about 1 km wide. The sharper one (VIIRS) sees squares about 375 m wide, so it notices smaller fires. When it joined in 2012, counts leapt up even though no more land burned. Parallax Fire lines the two up on one scale so the years can be compared fairly.

What it does not do

  • It does not predict future fires. It estimates what the sharper satellite would probably have counted in the past.
  • A detection is a hot pixel seen from space. It is not a confirmed wildfire.
  • Missing is never shown as zero. Estimates are always labelled and drawn dashed.

4. Two satellites and the jump

MODIS looks at the ground in squares about 1 km wide. It has been flying since 2000 and 2002 (Terra and Aqua). VIIRS sees squares about 375 m wide, so it notices smaller fires. It has been flying since 2012.

Gluing the two records together makes the line leap upward in 2012. The "Two satellites" page shows that jump twice: raw, and on one scale (the estimate).

The chart has a switch between squeezed and even scale. Squeezed gives every tripling the same height, so quiet and huge months can be read together, and zero stays at the bottom. Even gives equal steps equal counts, which is honest about size but makes quiet months look tiny.

The lines

  • Older satellite: what MODIS counted. Terra and Aqua are shown separately; Terra is never mixed into the estimate.
  • Sharper satellite: what VIIRS counted, since 2012.
  • Estimate (dashed): what the sharper satellite would probably have counted, with a shaded likely range.
  • Fixed heat sources: industry, kilns and similar, counted separately.

5. How the estimate is made

  1. Count the hot pixels. For each place and month we count the fire detections of each satellite.
  2. Notice the jump. The sharper satellite sees smaller fires, so counts leap when it joins.
  3. Learn the translation. In 2013–2020 both satellites were flying. We study how many detections the sharper one made whenever the older one saw a given number. These are the years used to learn (34,803,042 cell-months).
  4. Try several methods and pick one. Each was compared on 2021–2022, years held back for choosing (10,972,320 cell-months). The winner is the Tree model.
  5. Check it on unseen years. 2023–2024 were kept sealed until the end and used once (10,972,320 cell-months). The results in chapter 6 come from those years only.
  6. Add a likely range. Next to every estimate we show a likely range (8 in 10). Chapter 6 measures how often the real value landed inside it.
  7. Fill in the past. For 2003–2012, when only the older satellite existed, we show the estimated past, always dashed and labelled.

The methods compared

Error is the average miss of the monthly total over the whole pool of checked places, on the years used to choose (lower is better). A method only counts if it beats the simple rules by at least 10% (the gate rule).

7. Live data and the Earth view

The Live page uses real, current feeds only. Each layer shows its source, a time and an honest state: loading, none in the area, or feed unavailable.

Fire detections, last 24 hoursNASA FIRMS near-real-time files for MODIS, NOAA-20 VIIRS and NOAA-21 VIIRS, worldwide. Fetched through a server function and cached about 15 to 30 minutes.
Satellite imageryNASA GIBS true-colour images of the day before, and night lights.
Satellites nowTerra, Aqua, Suomi NPP, NOAA-20 and NOAA-21, calculated in your browser from public orbit elements (CelesTrak), refreshed no more often than every 2 hours.
BasemapOpenFreeMap (OpenStreetMap data). If it cannot load, a plain outline map is used and the page says so.

What "near-real-time" means

These detections arrive within a few hours of the overpass and are not final. They are not part of the monthly calendar, which uses the yearly FIRMS country files.

Suomi NPP feed

At the time of checking, NASA's Suomi NPP near-real-time file contained only its header and no detections. The page then says "none reported" for that satellite. It never shows an empty feed as zero fires.

Ground cameras are not embedded because their licences do not allow it. The satellite imagery is the "camera", and is labelled as imagery.

9. Limits and honest use

  • It does not say what will happen next. It lines up counts from the past and the present.
  • A detection is a hot pixel seen from space. It is not a confirmed wildfire and gives neither size nor damage.
  • Clouds and smoke can hide fires. FIRMS gives no count of cloud-free looks, so we cannot say how many fires were hidden.
  • The estimate cannot recover small fires the older satellite never saw. It tells you what the sharper satellite would typically have counted, with a range.
  • Results hold per place. A good result for one place says nothing about another.
  • With 24 checked months per place, the results are coarse evidence (uncertain by about 8% on coverage).
  • Fixed heat sources such as industry are counted separately. Crop and prescribed burning cannot be told apart from other vegetation fires and are included.
  • Months with no satellite record in this snapshot (Aqua / Terra / S-NPP): Australia 18 / 0 / 132; Bangladesh 18 / 2 / 132; United States 18 / 0 / 132. The S-NPP figure includes every month before it flew.
  • This snapshot is real data, not demo data.
